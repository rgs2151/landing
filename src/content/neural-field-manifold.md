Manifold geometry has become a powerful framework for understanding how the brain organizes high-dimensional neural activity into compact, interpretable structure. To date, however, most theoretical and methodological advances in neural manifold analysis have focused on spiking activity, leaving unresolved a fundamental question: what is the intrinsic geometric structure of local field potentials (LFPs)—population-level signals that reflect coordinated neural dynamics and are widely translatable in clinical and brain–computer interface settings. This question is challenging because standard population-manifold reconstruction methods rely on assumptions that are violated by LFPs, including strong temporal autocorrelation across channels. Here, we show that these very properties enable an analytical solution. Modeling LFPs as bounded autoregressive dynamical systems, we derive an explicit analytical characterization of their intrinsic geometry and prove that each sustained oscillatory component contributes an independent topological degree of freedom. As a result, the lag-embedded dynamics lie on a low-dimensional K-torus, where K is determined by the number of distinct oscillatory modes present in the signal. Aperiodic 1/f-like components and stochastic variability do not alter this topology, contributing only geometric thickness around the manifold. To handle nonstationarity and deviations from fixed linear dynamics in real data, we further introduce a geometry-aware learning network that parameterizes time-varying autoregressive operators, enabling robust recovery of the predicted manifold structure under realistic conditions. We validate these predictions using synthetic systems with known dynamics and demonstrate that the predicted toroidal geometry is recoverable in practice via lag embedding. Applying the framework to real neural recordings, we demonstrate its applicability across multiple, independent electrophysiological datasets forming a cross-species evaluation, with consistent recovery of the predicted manifold structure. Together, these results position intrinsic manifold reconstruction as a framework for probing the dynamical systems underlying local field potentials, with implications for mechanistic modeling and behavioral inference.
